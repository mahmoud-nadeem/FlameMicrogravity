### [CHUNK T3-05] PSI-101 SAME-R: objectives, hypothesis, approach, impacts (NASA text) | source: S2 | tags: objectives, hypothesis, approach, impacts
Objective [SRC]: Similar to the original SAME investigation, the primary objective of the SAME-R investigation was to quantify the sizes that the liquid smoke particulate achieves in low gravity and to relate the droplet size distribution to the smoke generation and smoke transport conditions in the experiment. One of the problems encountered during the SAME investigation was that after the thermal precipitators were returned to the ground, it was found that all the TEM grids were severely contaminated with some unidentified foreign particles, making these grids unusable although some of them showed shapes of certain types of smoke particles. Based on that experience the SAME-R investigation employed several modification covering new materials, extended operating conditions, and diagnostic equipments.
Hypothesis [SRC]: The three hypotheses that motivated the original SAME investigation also apply to SAME-R (see the SAME hypothesis chunk): microgravity smoke is expected to grow larger particles, which may change smoke detector response.
Approach [SRC]: SAME-R utilized the SAME hardware used on-orbit. Based on the experience gained from SAME, SAME-R included the following modifications: 1) a new test material, Pyrell, an intumescent fire-retardant foam by Foamex, was introduced to replace DBP samples, 2) test conditions were broadened including higher heating temperatures and no-flow during sample heating time, and 3) a new instrument, M-pass, developed in-house GRC, was used to replace one of the two Commercial Diagnostic Enclosures that includes a DustTrak and an ionization detector used in SAME. SAME-R flight tests were conducted from July 16, to September 9, 2010, successfully completing 66 test points. After SAME-R flight tests, three major post-flight ground tests were performed to improve data reduction process and to validate the lognormal distribution assumption, the key to the moment method employed by SAME and SAME-R.
Research impacts / Earth benefits [SRC]: Along with the results from the original SAME investigation, the results from SAME-R can lead to technology for an advanced fire detector for future spacecraft that will be used for long duration missions, and provide the reduced gravity baseline data against which future detection technology developments can be evaluated.
Team summary in plain words: Determine the sizes and size distributions of smoke particles in microgravity and relate them to how the smoke is generated and transported, while improving particle-shape data and reducing measurement uncertainty. It repeats SAME because the SAME samples came back from space contaminated with foreign particles, which limited their use. The SAME hardware was reused with improved sampling and diagnostic units. Five materials (Teflon, Kapton, cotton lampwick, silicone rubber and Pyrell) were heated below their ignition temperature, and the particles were measured with aerosol instruments and collected for electron microscopy.

### [CHUNK T3-08] Core concepts, glossary and equations (LaTeX) | source: S1, S2, S7, S12, S13 | tags: glossary, equations, theory, microgravity, aerosol
- Microgravity: the condition in which people and objects appear weightless; gravity on the ISS is still about 90% of Earth's; objects float because they are in free fall. [SRC S12]
- Without buoyancy hot gas does not rise, so smoke stays near its source and moves only with the air flow. [SRC S1 hypothesis]
- Smoke is an aerosol: liquid or solid particles suspended in air, with a spread of sizes. Growth mechanisms named by NASA: nucleation, condensation, coagulation. [SRC S1]
- Moments of a number size distribution n(D) (standard aerosol definition): $$M_k = \int_0^\infty D^k\, n(D)\, dD$$ M0 is the number concentration, M1 the first moment. [INF]
- Number-mean (arithmetic mean) diameter used in T3 (workbook column 'M1/M0 (micro-m)--AMD'): $$\bar D_{10} = M_1 / M_0$$ [SRC S7 column name]
- Lognormal size distribution with geometric mean (count median) diameter $D_g$ and geometric standard deviation $\sigma_g$; Hatch-Choate relations (standard): $$\bar D_{10} = D_g\, e^{\frac{1}{2}\ln^2\sigma_g}, \qquad D_{\text{avg mass}} = D_g\, e^{\frac{3}{2}\ln^2\sigma_g}$$ [INF]
- Check on the NASA workbook: for 124 flight tests with $\sigma_g$ and $D_g$, the two relations reproduce the columns 'M1/M0' (124 of 124) and 'Diameter of Average mass' (124 of 124) within 2%. So the workbook diameters are lognormal moment-method estimates. [CALC]
- Moment method: NASA inferred sizes from instrument averages (number and mass) assuming a lognormal distribution; SAME-R ground tests were run to validate that assumption. [SRC S2]
- Aging: the same smoke kept in the chamber and measured again later (test 'NN' fresh, 'NNa' aged). [SRC S5 Description column][CALC]
- Rt/R0: heater setting written as a resistance ratio; higher values go with higher sample temperatures in the data. Exact definition to be confirmed in the SAME engineering documents. [CALC][FLAG]
- Pyrolysis / overheating: SAME and SAME-R made smoke by heating samples, not by flaming fires. [SRC S1 approach]
- TEM: transmission electron microscope; thermophoretic samplers (thermal precipitators) collected particles for it; SAME's TEM grids came back contaminated. [SRC S1, S2]
- Instruments: P-Trak (condensation nuclei counter, pt/cc), DustTrak (mass concentration, mg/m3), ionization detectors Ion A and Ion B, the ISS smoke detector (scatter and obscuration channels), a detector labelled STS, and M-pass (GRC instrument that replaced one Commercial Diagnostic Enclosure in SAME-R). [SRC S2, S7, S8]
- Glovebox: SAME hardware was installed in the ISS Glovebox with a Far Field Box attached. [SRC S1 approach]

### [CHUNK T3-25] Cross-source synthesis (what the T3 data say together) | source: all | tags: insights, synthesis
1. Smoke from overheated materials keeps changing after it forms: the same smoke grew about 1.5x in mean diameter after 4-33 minutes (F-T3-1). A detector far from a source in still air would see older, larger smoke. [CALC][INF]
2. Weak air flow went with larger particles for three of five materials at the same heater setting (F-T3-2), consistent with NASA's expectation of longer residence time near the source in microgravity (S1). [CALC][INF]
3. Material matters about fourfold (57 nm Kapton to 207-244 nm Pyrell and Silicone at baseline, F-T3-3), but each material was heated to a different temperature, so material and temperature are mixed. [CALC]
4. Instruments set the limits of what can be concluded: the ISS detector's scatter channel pins at its range end in about half the tests (F-T3-6, F-T3-15); the P-Trak cannot see above 2.5 um; DAFT clocks drift (F-T3-20). [SRC][CALC]
5. Count and mass readings can disagree about the same aerosol (F-T3-13, F-T3-21), which matters because detectors respond to different particle properties. [CALC][INF]
6. Provenance needs care: table vs flight-record mismatch (F-T3-8), duplicated test number (F-T3-7), DBP without sizes (F-T3-9), a mislabelled housekeeping column (F-T3-19), six size rows that break a law of size statistics (F-T3-23, kept out; no conclusion changed). [CALC]

### [CHUNK T3-27] Limits: what the T3 data cannot answer | source: S1, S2, S3, team | tags: limitations, scope, out-of-scope
The smoke data in this track come from small samples of five spacecraft materials heated inside a glovebox on the International Space Station in 2007 (SAME) and 2010 (SAME-R), plus two short particle-counter checks in 2005-2006 (DAFT). All tests ran in normal station air, so the data say nothing directly about higher oxygen, lower pressure or the Moon's partial gravity. The smoke came from overheated, not burning, material. Particle sizes are NASA's estimates from instrument averages, assuming a typical size spread, and many comparisons rest on one to four tests. The two flights used partly different instruments and cannot be pooled. Detector timings reflect how the hardware carried smoke to each sensor, not how fast a detector would react to a real fire. Some instruments hit the end of their range (the station smoke detector's scatter signal, the counter's 500,000 particles/cm3 limit), and the DAFT instrument clocks drift, so second-by-second comparisons between instruments are uncertain.
- Not in the analysed data: oxygen above cabin air, low cabin pressure, Moon or Mars partial gravity, flaming fires, detector alarm thresholds, ground (1 g) comparison rows. [CALC]

### [CHUNK T3-29] Plain-language paragraph (for the site and the demo) | source: team | tags: plain-language, summary
Materials that overheat inside a spacecraft give off smoke even without flames, and smoke detectors are how the crew finds out. On Earth smoke rises; in microgravity it hangs near its source and moves only with the air. NASA's SAME (2007) and SAME-R (2010) experiments heated five spacecraft materials on the International Space Station and measured the smoke particles, and DAFT first checked that the particle counter works without gravity. The flight data show that this smoke keeps growing as it ages, grows larger when the air barely moves, and differs about fourfold in size between materials, while one channel of the station's own smoke detector appears to hit the end of its range in about half the tests. These are the facts a smoke-detection design for future spacecraft and Moon habitats has to start from.

### [CHUNK T3-QA] Q&A pairs (retrieval testing and few-shot) | source: all | tags: qa
Q: Does smoke from overheated spacecraft materials change as it ages in microgravity? A: Yes. In 42 of 44 paired SAME and SAME-R flight tests the number-mean particle diameter grew after 4 to 33 minutes of aging, by a median factor of 1.46 (F-T3-1).
Q: Which flown material gave the smallest smoke particles? A: Kapton, about 57 nm at its baseline (4 tests); Pyrell and Silicone gave the largest, about 207 to 244 nm. Each material was heated to its own baseline temperature (F-T3-3).
Q: Does weaker ventilation change the smoke? A: For Kapton, Lampwick and Silicone, runs with 0 to 2 cm/s air flow gave larger particles than runs at 8 cm/s at the same heater setting; Pyrell and Teflon did not. Groups are small (1 to 4 tests) (F-T3-2).
Q: Can the 2007 SAME and the 2010 SAME-R results be combined? A: Not without a correction: in all 5 matching comparisons SAME gave larger diameters, and SAME-R changed one instrument (F-T3-5).
Q: Did the modified P-Trak particle counter work in microgravity? A: NASA's DAFT post-flight report says it was functioning normally; it cannot see particles above 2.5 um and saturates at 500,000 particles/cm3 (F-T3-14, F-T3-22).
Q: Would a spacecraft smoke detector alarm in time for a fire in a Moon habitat at 30 % oxygen and 10.2 psi? A: Not in our data. SAME, SAME-R and DAFT ran in ISS cabin air: no oxygen or pressure changes, no partial gravity, and no alarm thresholds in these tables. The detector timings we have reflect how the hardware moved smoke (F-T3-16).
Q: Does smoke from a flaming fire behave the same way? A: Not in our data. These tests made smoke by overheating material samples, not by flaming fires (PSI-102 page, Approach).
Q: What are the PSI IDs and DOIs of the T3 experiments? A: SAME PSI-102 (10.60555/gd4k-8h62), SAME-R PSI-101 (10.60555/2np7-jv14), DAFT / DAFT-2 PSI-47 (10.60555/ztgj-d202). [SRC]
Q: Why was SAME flown again as SAME-R? A: SAME's TEM grids came back contaminated with foreign particles; SAME-R added Pyrell, wider conditions (higher heating, no-flow heating) and the M-pass instrument. [SRC S2]
Q: Which materials were tested? A: SAME: Teflon, Kapton, silicone rubber, lampwick (cotton) and DBP; SAME-R: Kapton, Teflon, lampwick, silicone and Pyrell (DBP replaced). [SRC]
Q: How was particle size measured? A: By the moment method: instrument averages of number and mass combined under a lognormal size assumption; the workbook diameters satisfy the Hatch-Choate relations (T3-08). [SRC][CALC]
Q: What does 'aged' mean in these tests? A: The same smoke kept in the chamber and measured again later (test 'NN' fresh, 'NNa' aged). [SRC][CALC]
Q: Did the ISS smoke detector saturate? A: Its scatter channel bottoms out near -6.25 V and stays there in 21 of 44 SAME-R tests; likely the end of its range, not yet confirmed in documents (F-T3-6, F-T3-15). [CALC][INF]
Q: How long did smoke take to reach the sensors? A: About two minutes after heater start (median 129-139 s), usually after heating had ended; this is a hardware transport time (F-T3-16). [CALC]
Q: Which sensor responded first? A: The ISS scatter channel in 32 of 41 tests, DustTrak B in 9; low confidence because the sensors sit at different points of the flow path (F-T3-17). [CALC]
Q: Is there oxygen or pressure data? A: No. All T3 tests ran in ISS cabin air; the tables have no oxygen or cabin-pressure columns. [CALC]
Q: Can the DAFT instruments be compared second by second? A: Both logged in all 7 tests, but their clocks drift (report p.5), so second-by-second alignment is uncertain by tens of seconds (F-T3-11, F-T3-20). [SRC][CALC]
Q: Was the data cleaned before the findings? A: Yes, without changing any NASA file: housekeeping rows with repeated timestamps and a repeated header line were skipped; rows without a size (20 DBP rows, 3 runs without a P-Trak count) and 6 size rows that break a law of size statistics were kept out of the size analyses (F-T3-23); NASA's zero-filled DAFT gaps were excluded; a P-Trak reading above the instrument maximum was kept and flagged (F-T3-22). Every step is listed with file and row in each record's data_log.md. [CALC]
Q: What are the biggest data traps? A: Pooling SAME and SAME-R, using the SAME experimental table instead of the flight records, and treating the housekeeping DustTrakA column as mg/m3 (T3-26). [CALC]
